It is humbling to see what nature leaves behind after a living thing has reached the end of its life.

Here’s a look at a few examples and how these remarkable patterns form.

> Art forms in nature in unexpectedly beautiful ways leaving behind a trace of life that existed for a brief moment.

## Sea urchin

From a spiny marine animal to something resembling a piece of patterned porcelain.

An urchin’s spines are held on by tiny muscles, so once the animal dies they drop off within days, leaving the bare shell to wash ashore. Its five-part pattern is the same one you’ll find in a starfish. The living animal has a second life on the plate: its orange roe is the uni of sushi menus.

## Cholla cactus

Its woody support structure survives as an elaborate, perforated lattice.

When a cholla dies in the desert, the sun dries out its fleshy outer layer and it crumbles away, leaving only the woody tube that held the plant up. People across the American Southwest collect these skeletons and turn them into lamps, furniture and walking sticks.

## Lunaria

The seed pod opens, leaving a delicate, silvery membrane.

Its flat, coin-shaped pods have earned it names like money plant and silver dollar. As a pod dries, the two outer husks peel away and the seeds fall, leaving only the paper-thin silver partition that sat between them. That is why gardeners cut the stems and dry them for winter bouquets.

## Chinese Lantern

The papery orange layer loses its tissue, leaving a fine network of veins around the fruit.

The lantern is a sheath that grows around the berry as it ripens, turning from green to bright orange. Left out through autumn and winter, rain and frost break down the soft tissue between the veins, and the lacy skeleton is what’s left, often with the berry still inside. In Japan, where it is called hozuki, bunches are placed on altars at Obon as lanterns to guide ancestral spirits home.

## Sand Dollar

The velvety spines disappear, revealing a pale skeleton with a defined five-petalled pattern.

A living sand dollar is covered in thousands of tiny spines that work like legs, shuffling it across the sand and passing food toward its mouth. After it dies they fall off and the sun bleaches the shell white. Crack one open and five small pieces rattle out, its jaw, which folklore calls the doves of peace.

## Lotus Seed Pod

Once the petals fall and the seed head matures, it dries into a sculptural object.

Each hole holds a single seed, which loosens and rattles free as the pod dries. Lotus seeds can stay alive for centuries: some recovered from an ancient lakebed in China sprouted after about 1,300 years. Across Asia nearly every part of the plant is eaten, from the seeds and roots to the leaves, and the dried pods are a favourite in flower arrangements.

## Driftwood

Water and weather gradually wear away the wood's surface, revealing curves and grain.

It takes years. Sun bleaches the surface silver-grey, and water and sand wear away the softer wood between the growth rings faster than the harder rings, which is what carves those ridges and swirls. Artists collect driftwood for sculptures, tables and mirror frames.

## Skeleton Leaf

As the softer parts of a leaf disappear, its delicate network of veins can remain.

In nature, bacteria, fungi and insects slowly eat the soft green tissue and leave the tougher veins behind. You can do the same at home by soaking sturdy leaves in water for weeks until the flesh brushes away, a craft used for bookmarks, jewellery and paintings.
