It is humbling to see what nature leaves behind after a living thing has reached the end of its life.

Here’s a look at a few examples and how these remarkable patterns form.

> Nothing here was made to be admired. The softest parts simply fall away, and what is built to last stays.

## Sea urchin

From a spiny marine animal to something resembling a piece of patterned porcelain.

The shell, called a test, is built from interlocking plates of calcium carbonate in five repeating segments. The small bumps are where the spines once attached, and the paired pores along each segment are where its tube feet reached out.

## Cholla cactus

Its woody support structure survives as an elaborate, perforated lattice.

The lattice is lignified vascular tissue that once carried water and nutrients through the plant. In the American Southwest the dried skeletons are collected as cholla wood and made into lamps, furniture and crafts.

## Lunaria

The seed pod opens, leaving a delicate, silvery membrane.

Also called honesty, the plant takes its name from that see-through partition, which holds the seeds in place until the outer layers fall away. Lunaria comes from the Latin for moon.

## Chinese Lantern

The papery orange layer loses its tissue, leaving a fine network of veins around the fruit.

That orange lantern is the calyx, which grows to wrap the berry as it ripens. In Japan, where it is known as hozuki, the lanterns are placed on altars at Obon to guide ancestral spirits home.

## Sand Dollar

The velvety spines disappear, revealing a pale skeleton with a defined five-petalled pattern.

A flattened relative of the sea urchin, the living animal is covered in fine spines that it uses to move across sand. Left in the sun, the empty test bleaches white, and the petal pattern marks the rows of pores it once breathed through.

## Lotus Seed Pod

Once the petals fall and the seed head matures, it dries into a sculptural object.

Each hole once held a single seed. Lotus seeds are famously long-lived: seeds recovered from an ancient lakebed in China have been germinated after about 1,300 years.

## Driftwood

Water and weather gradually wear away the wood's surface, revealing curves and grain.

Sun breaks down the lignin in the surface layer, which is why old wood turns silver-grey. The softer parts of each growth ring wear away faster than the harder parts, which brings out the ridges you see.

## Skeleton Leaf

As the softer parts of a leaf disappear, its delicate network of veins can remain.

The veins are made of tougher, lignin-rich tissue that resists decay long after the softer tissue between them has broken down. Once, that network carried water into the leaf and sugars out of it.
