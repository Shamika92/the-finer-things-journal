Sitting in a café one evening, I found myself lost in which coffee to choose. I like to try different varieties in new places, but this menu felt endless; I have been experiencing this a lot lately. It made me think of simpler times, when we just ordered a frothy cup of Nescafé or Bru.

Step into a modern speciality café, and the menu often reads like a wine list. Beans are not simply described as strong or mild, but the bags list the country, farm altitude, botanical variety, and processing method, along with flavour notes such as peach, jasmine, citrus, or toasted nuts.

<p>Borrowing the language of wine has encouraged drinkers to see coffee as a craft product shaped by soil, climate, and harvest decisions.</p>
</blockquote>

It turns a routine morning beverage into a precious cup that needs more attention to details.

## Coffee as an Accessible Luxury

A person may not be able to afford designer clothes, a beautiful apartment, or frequent international holidays. But they can surely afford a neatly presented coffee in a thoughtfully designed space.

<blockquote class="quiet">
<p>Coffee offers temporary access to an aspirational lifestyle.</p>
</blockquote>

This reflects the idea of *accessible luxury*: a small discretionary purchase that makes everyday life feel more pleasurable or refined. For the price of a single cup, a customer gets to sit in a sunny room, drink from handmade ceramics, and spend a leisurely hour in a well-curated space, enjoying good music and the smell of roasted beans, all without the cost of ownership.

## Before Coffee Had Its Waves

But this is not a new idea; long before speciality menus and single-origin labels existed, coffee already played an important role in social and domestic life.

Across the Arabian Peninsula, pouring cardamom-infused *qahwa* from a long-beaked *dallah* was an established gesture of welcome and mutual respect. As coffee spread through the Ottoman Empire, the *kahvehane* became a regular gathering place where merchants, poets, and townspeople met to talk, read, and play chess.

Nineteenth-century Viennese coffeehouses developed a similar rhythm, where buying a single cup allowed writers and regulars to spend hours reading newspapers in quiet company. In South India, meanwhile, coffee took root as a daily domestic ritual, with morning filter *kaapi*, frothed between a brass *davara* and tumbler, becoming a familiar part of Tamil middle-class family life.

Across all these settings, coffee helped establish what sociologists call a **third place**: a social environment separate from home and work, where people could spend time without feeling rushed.

Contemporary cafés offer an interesting variation of this idea. We don't necessarily visit them to socialise. A 2021 study titled *Lone Not Lonely*, which examined people visiting specialty cafés alone, found that the atmosphere and presence of other people could contribute to a sense of belonging even without direct social interaction.

These waves do not particularly replace one another. A person may drink instant coffee before work, order a cappuccino while meeting a friend and enjoy preparing a single-origin coffee over the weekend. Each serves a different purpose in their life.

## The Wine-ification of Coffee

At the same time, specialized vocabulary brings new social hierarchies. Knowing how to order a washed single-origin pour-over becomes a subtle marker of taste and cultural capital. The line between genuine appreciation and snobbery can thin quickly, especially when simpler preferences, such as taking coffee with milk and sugar, start feeling unfashionable.

There is also a practical economic question behind the storytelling. Specialty coffee is priced much higher than commercial blends, and direct-trade roasters have helped many smallholder farmers earn better returns. Still, a large share of an expensive cup goes toward city rents, equipment, packaging, and labor, so higher menu prices do not automatically mean the grower is taking home the difference.

## The Coffee Connoisseur vs. the Café Connoisseur

Today's café culture brings together two different kinds of enthusiasts whose habits overlap on the surface.

The **coffee connoisseur** cares primarily about what is inside the cup.

The **café connoisseur**, by contrast, is drawn mainly to the space around the cup.

This second preference has helped shape a recognizable global café aesthetic. Walk into an independent café in Seoul, Copenhagen, Tokyo, Melbourne, or Mumbai, and you will often find similar visual details: pale wood, neutral walls, minimalist menus, and clean glassware. Across very different cities, these rooms offer a familiar sense of calm and very similar playlists.

## Our Inspiration from Cinema and Television

Everyday lifestyle aspirations rarely form in isolation; they are often shaped by what we see on screen. Over the past few decades, film, television, and social media have turned coffee into a recurring visual cue for a particular way of living.

In Western television and cinema, from the neighborhood coffee shop in *Friends* and Luke’s Diner in *Gilmore Girls* to long conversations across café tables in European films, the café served as a backdrop for adult independence and long conversation.

More recently, South Korean dramas and lifestyle media have popularized a softer, quieter café aesthetic: sunlit wooden tables, gentle background music, and an iced Americano in hand, whatever the season.

This creates an interesting contradiction. Biologically, coffee is a stimulant meant to increase alertness and keep people productive. Culturally, however, screens and social media consistently use a warm cup by a window as shorthand for slowing down and taking a quiet pause.

## India's Journey from Chai to Café Culture

India is still very much a tea country, though it has a long and varied history with coffee.

In South India, filter *kaapi* has been a staple of home life for generations, valued for its strong chicory-blended decoction and frothy milk. Across cities from Kolkata and Delhi to Bengaluru, the **Indian Coffee House** offered an affordable, unpretentious room where students, writers, and office workers could sit for hours over simple filter coffee and conversation.

In the late 1990s and 2000s, **Café Coffee Day** opened a new chapter for a growing urban middle class. Its air-conditioned, casual outlets gave young people and friends an approachable place to meet and linger without pressure to leave quickly.

Today's Indian specialty coffee movement builds on those earlier habits while turning attention toward homegrown estates in Chikmagalur, Coorg, and the Araku Valley. Local roasters now present Indian beans with modern packaging and design-led cafés.

However, this growth has not replaced *chai*, instead the two occupy different moments of the day: a quick roadside *chai* during a busy afternoon, and a slower sit-down coffee when there is time to spare.

## A Global Culture, a Local Cup

Global trends tend to make cities look more and more the same, yet food and drink are rarely completely devoid of their local character. You can have the most generic little specialist café in the world with the same interior from one end of the world to the other and the menu will still be very much rooted in the region. You'll have cardamom coffee, Vietnamese egg coffee, South Indian filter coffee served in modern glass and more.

Fine wine has always been defined by rarity and exclusion. Coffee has borrowed its language of origin and craft while staying within reach. Whether it is a single-origin pour-over in Seoul or filter *kaapi* in Bengaluru, the appeal remains quietly democratic.
