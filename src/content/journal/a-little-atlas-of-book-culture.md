<blockquote class="poem">
<p>There is no Frigate like a Book
To take us Lands away</p>
<cite>Emily Dickinson, <em>There is no Frigate like a Book</em></cite>
</blockquote>

People have savoured books for centuries, sharing stories and keeping company with great minds. But beyond reading them, cultures have also turned books into small rituals, habits and even words, each one a different way of exploring the world of books.

Here are five of them.

<figure>
  <div class="grain"><img src="/images/journal/a-little-atlas-of-book-culture/reading.jpg" alt="Fragonard’s painting of a young girl in a yellow dress, seen in profile, reading a small book" loading="lazy" /></div>
</figure>

## Chaekgeori

<p class="meta">South Korea</p>

Chaekgeori, which roughly means “books and things”, is a style of Korean painting that shows shelves filled with books and precious objects.

A masterclass in optical illusion from the Joseon era, where entire walls were painted to look like towering, three-dimensional bookshelves.

<figure class="wide">
  <div class="grain"><img src="/images/journal/a-little-atlas-of-book-culture/korea.jpg" alt="A chaekgeori painting: shelves with deep blue backs, filled with stacked books, vases, scholars’ tools and bowls of fruit" loading="lazy" /></div>
</figure>

<blockquote class="quiet">
<p>Stuffed with precious manuscripts, scholars' tools, and symbolic fruits, it celebrated the mind as life’s greatest luxury.</p>
</blockquote>

The style took off in the late 1700s under King Jeongjo, a devoted book collector who encouraged scholarship, and screens like this were reportedly placed behind the royal throne. Scholar families soon followed, and by the 1800s it had reached ordinary homes as a more decorative folk art.

Look closely and the shelves and objects are often seen from slightly different angles at once, which adds to the dreamlike effect.

## Tsundoku

<p class="meta">Japan</p>

Born as 19th-century Japanese slang for letting bought books pile up unread.

<figure>
  <div class="grain"><img src="/images/journal/a-little-atlas-of-book-culture/japan.jpg" alt="A secondhand bookshop in Tokyo’s Kanda-Jimbocho district, its shelves of books open to the street under green awnings" loading="lazy" /></div>
</figure>

<blockquote class="quiet">
<p>It affectionately captures the gap between a reader’s boundless curiosity and the harsh reality of finite time.</p>
</blockquote>

The word blends *tsunde-oku*, to pile things up and leave them, with *doku*, to read. It is usually traced back to 1879, when it appeared in a satire about a teacher with unread books on his shelves.

## Les Bouquinistes

<p class="meta">France</p>

An open-air tradition running along the river since the 1500s.

<figure>
  <div class="grain"><img src="/images/journal/a-little-atlas-of-book-culture/france.jpg" alt="Green bouquiniste stalls along the Seine in Paris, beneath tall plane trees, with browsers on the pavement" loading="lazy" /></div>
</figure>

Behind hundreds of weathered green wooden boxes, vendors sell rare paperbacks, vintage postcards, and forgotten classics directly over the water.

Their boxes line roughly three kilometres of the Seine, and the riverbanks they sit on are part of Paris’s UNESCO World Heritage listing.

## Jólabókaflóð

<p class="meta">Iceland</p>

*(pronounced yo-la-bo-ka-flowth)*

Jólabókaflóð, or the “Christmas book flood”, is Iceland’s much-loved holiday tradition of giving and reading books.

<figure>
  <div class="grain"><img src="/images/journal/a-little-atlas-of-book-culture/iceland.jpg" alt="An illustration of a wicker basket holding a knitted blanket and a wrapped red book, beside a mug of hot chocolate topped with cream and cinnamon" loading="lazy" /></div>
</figure>

Each autumn, new books flood the market, and on Christmas Eve, people exchange books and spend the rest of the night reading them while drinking hot chocolate.

The tradition is often traced back to the Second World War, when paper was one of the few things not rationed in Iceland. Every November, a free catalogue of new titles, the *Bókatíðindi*, is delivered to homes across the country, and the shopping begins.

## El Ateneo Grand Splendid

<p class="meta">Argentina</p>

A historic 1919 theatre in Buenos Aires where book aisles replace audience seats, and the grand stage is now a café where you can read under massive frescoed ceilings.

<figure>
  <div class="grain"><img src="/images/journal/a-little-atlas-of-book-culture/el-ateneo.jpg" alt="Looking down inside El Ateneo Grand Splendid: curved tiers of balconies lined with books, and a round, glowing stairwell in the middle of the old theatre floor" loading="lazy" /></div>
</figure>

It first opened as the Teatro Gran Splendid, staging opera, ballet and tango. Later it became a cinema, and in 2000 it was reborn as a bookshop. The stage, the balconies and the red curtains were all kept, which is why browsing here still feels a little like arriving for a show.
