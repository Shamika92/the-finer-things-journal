If you spend time around kids, watching their films and reading their stories, you cannot help but feel awe at how well they balance sadness, grief, and hope.

Children start their lives with their minds like an empty canvas. Stories, books, and their surroundings add different colours, forming a richer picture as they grow. Good literature becomes a quiet rehearsal for real-life feelings, helping young readers grasp complex human experiences before they have to live through them.

Yet these same tales also tug at the heartstrings of adults, those of us who have traded a child’s simple view of the world for its harsher realities. In fact, they often hit harder with age, carrying nuances and quiet grief that a child’s mind cannot yet fully grasp.

C.S. Lewis wrote in his essay “On Three Ways of Writing for Children”:

<blockquote class="long"><p>“I am almost inclined to set it up as a canon that a children’s story which is enjoyed only by children is a bad children’s story.”</p></blockquote>

Here are a few stories that remain simple in plot but age remarkably well, giving us a wider range of emotions alongside a warm wave of childhood nostalgia.

Ultimately, what stays with us from these stories is close to a Japanese concept of ‘*[Mono no aware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mono_no_aware)*,’ a gentle awareness of how fleeting life, beauty, and innocence truly are. It is an acceptance of the [transience of all things](https://berkleycenter.georgetown.edu/posts/mono-no-aware-the-transience-of-life) that heightens appreciation of their beauty, and evokes a gentle sadness at their passing.
